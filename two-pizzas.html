The two-pizza rule

Small teams ship faster.

A team should be small enough to be fed by two pizzas. Past that, communication starts costing more than the work itself.

What it means

If you can't feed your whole team with two pizzas, the team is too big. In practice that's roughly 4 to 8 people — engineers, a designer, a product owner, anyone who has to be in the room to make a decision.

The size of the team is not a HR detail. It's the most important architectural decision you make about how the team thinks.

Where it comes from

The rule is usually attributed to Jeff Bezos at Amazon in the early 2000s. As Amazon grew, more people in a room did not mean more output — it meant more meetings, more sign-offs, and slower decisions. Bezos pushed the company toward small, autonomous "two-pizza teams" that owned a service end-to-end.

Amazon eventually moved past the strict two-pizza framing toward "single-threaded" teams with one clear owner, but the underlying intuition — keep the team small enough that everyone fits around one table — stuck.

Why it works

Communication overhead grows faster than the team. With n people there are n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 possible pairings. Five people: 10 channels. Ten people: 45. Fifteen: 105. Doubling the team quadruples the coordination cost.

Less coordination

Fewer people means fewer meetings, fewer hand-offs, and fewer "who owns this?" loops.

Shared context

Everyone knows the codebase, the customer, and the trade-offs. Decisions don't need a kickoff doc.

Real ownership

A small team can hold a service end-to-end: design, build, deploy, on-call, support.

Faster feedback

Short loops between idea, code, and customer. Mistakes are cheap, so experiments are cheap.

Signs your team is too big

  • Standups run long and most updates aren't relevant to most people.
  • Decisions stall because "we should loop in X" — and X is on the team.
  • You need a project manager just to track who is doing what.
  • Sub-groups quietly form and stop talking to each other.
  • Onboarding takes weeks because no one person can explain the whole system.

How to apply it

  1. Cut along service or product lines, not skill lines. A two-pizza team owns a thing customers can name, not a layer of the stack.
  2. Give it a single, clear owner. One person who can say yes or no without escalating.
  3. Push autonomy down. The team decides its tech, process, and on-call rotation. If every choice needs approval, the size doesn't matter.
  4. Keep dependencies few and explicit. Two-pizza teams break when half their work is blocked on another team.
  5. When the team grows past two pizzas, split before it hurts. Splitting is cheap when ownership is clean and expensive when it isn't.

When it doesn't fit

Two-pizza teams aren't a universal answer. They work best when the team owns a self-contained slice of the product and can ship without waiting on others. They struggle when:

  • The work is inherently large and tightly coupled (a single shared platform, a regulated migration).
  • Specialised skills are scarce and have to be shared across many teams.
  • The organisation hasn't pushed real decision-making authority down — small teams without autonomy are just smaller bottlenecks.

The point isn't the pizza count. It's the principle: keep the smallest group that can own the outcome, and remove the things that stop them from owning it.
